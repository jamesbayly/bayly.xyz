# Writing for bayly.xyz

This is a working voice guide for drafting **James's blog essays**, not his professional copy. It is based on the 40 posts in `src/content/posts/` dated March 2025 to August 2026, with close reading across the archive. The 2026 pieces are the best default model for new work: they retain the bite of the earlier posts but tend to be tighter and more grounded. These are observed patterns, not a template every post must obey.

## What the article is doing

A place, object, journey, or encounter is the way into a bigger idea. The subject is rarely just *what it was like there*. It is the friction underneath: tourism changing work, money changing a city, infrastructure changing access, a border splitting people, a traveller discovering that their assumptions were wrong.

Pick **one claim worth testing** before drafting. Then let the physical place complicate it. [The Mountain That Changed Its Career](src/content/posts/ijen-mornings.md) is about a volcano, but really about sulphur mining becoming tourism. [Cities That Surprise Us](src/content/posts/cities-that-surprise-us.md) uses four cities to undo a lazy picture of Central Asia. [Go Now](src/content/posts/go-now.md) wants people to visit Kyrgyzstan while admitting that visitors help change what they came to see.

## The voice

- **An opinionated witness, not a neutral guide.** James will say when something is beautiful, absurd, cruel, seductive, or badly designed. He is willing to be wrong in public and to revise his first impression. See [Cities That Surprise Us](src/content/posts/cities-that-surprise-us.md) and [The Bewildering Logic of Travel](src/content/posts/bromo-traffic.md).
- **Concrete before abstract.** Begin with a street, a machine, a drink, a sound, a journey, or a small human action. Use details that could have come from being there: the one SIM-card kiosk, old men outside a village, a train platform, a Lada being repaired on cardboard. Let the idea grow out of those particulars. See [Pamir Highway](src/content/posts/pamir-orange-state.md), [Pamir: Lost Capitalism](src/content/posts/pamir-lost-capatalism.md), and [Iron Horses](src/content/posts/central-asian-cars.md).
- **Conversational, sharp, occasionally irreverent.** Short declarative sentences sit next to longer, rhythmic passages. A blunt aside, rhetorical question, or dry joke can puncture a grand claim. The humour is often directed at tourists, institutions, or James himself. It should sound like a perceptive traveller talking to a friend, not a tourism board or an academic abstract. See [Iron Horses](src/content/posts/central-asian-cars.md) and [The Emperor Who Refused to Die](src/content/posts/china-xian.md).
- **Critical without becoming detached.** The posts notice exploitation, waste, authoritarianism, and cultural change, but also pleasure, competence, hospitality, and contradiction. A useful turn is to implicate the narrator: he enjoys the comfort, takes the photo, buys the ticket, or benefits from the system he is questioning. See [Ella](src/content/posts/sri-lanka-ella.md), [Kawah Ijen](src/content/posts/ijen-mornings.md), and [Go Now](src/content/posts/go-now.md).
- **A vivid image, then move on.** Metaphor is part of the voice, but the recent work is strongest when the image clarifies a physical or political reality. Avoid stacking three metaphors where one precise observation would do. [Bukhara](src/content/posts/uzbekistan-bukhara.md) and [Khiva](src/content/posts/uzbekistan-khiva.md) are good references for the more restrained register.

## Shape and pacing

1. **Open on tension.** An immediate scene, a counterintuitive statement, or a piece of history that changes how we see the present. No generic travel preamble. [Khiva](src/content/posts/uzbekistan-khiva.md) opens with a disguised visitor in 1863; [The Emperor Who Refused to Die](src/content/posts/china-xian.md) starts with a bicycle ride; [Go Now](src/content/posts/go-now.md) opens with a closing window.
2. **Move between scales.** Step from the observed moment into history, economics, design, or politics, then back to a person or object in front of the narrator. Explain context only where it sharpens the central idea.
3. **Let the objection in.** If the argument is too neat, show the counterexample or the narrator's own complicity. Avoid a clean hero/villain split when the scene is messier.
4. **End with a turn, not a summary.** Return to the opening image, reveal its cost, or land one clear sentence that reframes the scene. Do not finish with a generic lesson about travel, a marketing call to action, or a recap of every point. See [Bukhara](src/content/posts/uzbekistan-bukhara.md), [Kawah Ijen](src/content/posts/ijen-mornings.md), and [Go Now](src/content/posts/go-now.md).

Most posts are compact: the archive median is about **575 body words** after excluding the gallery, though some narrative pieces are much longer. In 38 of 40 posts, the body has no subheadings before the gallery. Use paragraphs and pacing to carry a short essay; add headings only when the material genuinely needs them. One-sentence paragraphs are useful for a turn or punchline, not every other paragraph.

## Titles and presentation

Titles usually promise an **idea or tension**, not a comprehensive destination guide: [The Mountain That Changed Its Career](src/content/posts/ijen-mornings.md), [The Emperor Who Refused to Die](src/content/posts/china-xian.md), [The Bewildering Logic of Travel](src/content/posts/bromo-traffic.md). A location-led title can work when the second half supplies the angle, as in [Pamir Highway: The Orange State](src/content/posts/pamir-orange-state.md). Avoid SEO-listicle titles and stock adjectives like *hidden gem*.

Use New Zealand English, per James's standing preference; spelling in the archive is not fully consistent. His standing prose preference is **no em dashes**, even though some older posts contain them. Use commas, colons, parentheses, or sentence breaks. Keep the prose direct; do not copy typos or unverified factual claims from the archive as if they were part of the style.

## When helping James draft

- Get the raw material first: what he actually saw, heard, did, photographed, and felt; the observation that stayed with him; the argument he is tempted to make; and the bit that troubles that argument. If those details are missing, leave a question or a clearly marked placeholder. **Never invent first-hand experience, dialogue, people, or sensory details.**
- Draft a one-sentence thesis and a scene-to-idea arc before polishing sentences. Prefer a few memorable, specific details to a destination roundup.
- Preserve the bite. Do not smooth every provocative judgment into polite, generic balance. But distinguish observation from interpretation, keep people from becoming scenery, and substantiate consequential claims.
- Verify names, dates, historical anecdotes, statistics, laws, and current conditions against reliable sources before publication. The existing posts are evidence of **voice**, not automatically verified research. Label unresolved claims for James rather than laundering them into confident prose.
- Edit for excess: cut repeated imagery, rhetorical questions that do not change the argument, sweeping claims unsupported by the scene, and grand closing morals. Keep a few abrupt, funny, or uncomfortable lines when they earn their place.

**Quick test:** Could the opening have been written without going there? Does the middle reveal more than the opening scene? Does James's own position appear honestly? Does the final image or line change how we see the subject? If not, keep working.
