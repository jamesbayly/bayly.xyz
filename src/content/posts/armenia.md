I spent two days in Armenia, heading out of the city on tours each morning. This is not enough time to understand a country. It is, apparently, enough time to become quite unreasonable about its architectural heritage.

A medieval monastery in a spectacular mountain setting? Lovely.

Another one? Incredible.

Another one?

Armenia had spent centuries producing these places. I had given myself forty-eight hours to appreciate them, including travel time. Somewhere along the way, a thousand years of history and an exceptionally well-chosen cliff stopped being enough.

The problem wasn't that they weren't impressive. The problem was that they all were.

<!-- Photo: Noravank against the red cliffs. -->

[Noravank](https://armenia.travel/destination/noravank-monastery/) sits against towering red cliffs, the sort of setting that would make a reasonably ordinary building look magnificent. The medieval Armenians had put a magnificent building there instead. Overkill, frankly.

At [Sevanavank](https://sevan-park.am/en/2019/05/17/sevanavank/), the backdrop changes to Lake Sevan. Different landscape, more ancient churches. Even the scenery was working hard to keep the format fresh.

<!-- Photo: Sevanavank and Lake Sevan. -->

Geghard offers a further variation by being partly carved into the mountain itself. Most builders encounter a cliff and adjust the plans. Here, somebody had decided the cliff was the building material.

It deserves considerably more attention than "the one in the rock", which is the filing system your brain starts implementing when you compress a country into two days.

<!-- Photo: Geghard's rock-cut architecture. -->

Garni, at least, broke the pattern.

Columns. A pediment. Something that looks as though it belongs on the cover of a textbook about ancient Greece.

Thank God. A pagan.

The [classical temple at Garni](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/6808/) stands above the Azat gorge, alongside the remains of a royal complex that includes a bathhouse with a mosaic floor. Its columns look familiar, but the setting refuses to look Mediterranean. What is all this doing in the Caucasus?

<!-- Photo: Garni temple above the gorge. -->

For centuries, Rome and Parthia competed for influence over Armenia, including who got to occupy its throne. In AD 66, the Parthian prince Tiridates travelled to Rome to be [crowned king of Armenia by Nero](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/de/document/doi/10.1515/jah-2020-0030/html). One empire supplied the candidate; the other supplied the ceremony. Both could claim a result.

Garni is traditionally attributed to Tiridates I. Rather than a stray piece of Rome, it is a reminder of an Armenian royal court participating in a much wider classical world. The influences travelled further than the borders on a modern map suggest.

Armenia wasn't simply a blank space between empires. It had its own rulers and ambitions. Unfortunately, its neighbours also had ambitions for it.

The [longer history](https://online.ucpress.edu/jmw/article/1/2/77/50945/Locating-Medieval-Armenia-at-the-Metropolitan) changes the names: Roman and Persian powers, Byzantines, Arab rulers, Seljuks, Mongols, Ottomans. The ancient Armenian world extended beyond today's borders, but the recurring problem is recognisable. How do you keep a country, or a culture, intact when larger powers keep rearranging the region?

Which brings us, inevitably, back to the monasteries.

Armenia adopted Christianity in the early fourth century, [traditionally dated to 301](https://armenia.travel/about-armenia/the-first-christian-nation/). Over the centuries, its monasteries became much more than places to pray. [Geghard had a school, a library, and a scriptorium](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/960/). Its complex also included defensive walls and princely tombs. Education, religion, protection, and the interests of powerful families all occupied the same site.

There is rather more going on than a succession of attractive old churches.

<!-- Photo: Tatev and the surrounding gorge. -->

[Tatev had a university](https://armenia.travel/destination/tatev-monastery/), teaching subjects including mathematics, astronomy, philosophy, and languages. These institutions helped transmit knowledge through generations in which political authority was anything but dependable.

A king could lose his throne. A manuscript could still be copied. People could still be taught to read it.

I had been touring libraries, schools, family monuments, and religious institutions, then complaining about the lack of variety. Nobody had forced me onto these tours. I had paid for the privilege of becoming bored by them.

<!-- Photo: Dilijan, a visual break from the monastery sequence. -->

Dilijan gave the photographs a change of subject. It also deserves a place here for the simple reason that Armenia contains towns and people, not just dramatic locations where medieval monks had excellent taste in property.

It would be easy to turn the whole country into a monument to surviving other people's history. That is another way of making it smaller than it is.

But the modern map does make the older story harder to dismiss. For ordinary overland travel, [the borders with Turkey and Azerbaijan remain closed](https://travel.state.gov/en/international-travel/travel-advisories/armenia.html), leaving Georgia to the north and Iran to the south as Armenia's land connections to the outside world. The reasons belong to modern conflicts and politics, not some uninterrupted war stretching back to Nero. Still, the practical consequences of difficult neighbours are hardly confined to the history books.

The classical temple, the monasteries, and the closed borders aren't the same story. They are different chapters in a country repeatedly negotiating its place among more powerful neighbours.

Two days barely gave me time to notice the connections, let alone understand them. It gave me plenty of photographs.

Looking through them now, the monasteries are bloody good.

I might have appreciated them more if I'd given myself longer than a weekend to get through the last two thousand years.
