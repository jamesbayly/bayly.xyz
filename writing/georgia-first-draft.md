# The View North

The signboards were in Cyrillic but plastered with standard scenic photographs. Burly-looking men stood on the streets of Georgia selling tours, and the Russian buses were much cheaper than the English-speaking alternatives. So I bought a seat, opened Google Translate, and went sightseeing in a language I don't speak.

Now here I was, heading towards Gergeti in the far north of the country, near the Russian border, surrounded by Russian tourists, and with a tour guide who had only limited english. I had plenty of time to think.

This wasn't new. Over the previous months in Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, and Azerbaijan, I had kept finding the same thing: the easiest and cheapest way to see a place was often to join the tour built for Russian speakers and translate my way through it. The Soviet Union had gone. Russian remained a language of the tourist trade in these countries that bordered it on the south. So much so that you would usually be welcommed into a taxi with the Russian "privet".

I was taking advantage of that trade myself. It's hard to complain about a market while you're enjoying its discount.

Georgia made the arrangement feel different. In Tbilisi, the main street was lined with fancy shops. The city felt cosmopolitan and completely normal, in the best possible sense: somewhere you could have a lovely time without spending every minute thinking about geopolitics. Georgia was not a country waiting around to be defined by Russia.

But the road north has a way of bringing the subject back.

South Ossetia lies just west of the route through Gudauri towards Gergeti. We weren't going there. But the territory at the centre of the 2008 war sat just over the mountain, close enough to make this an interesting time.

The 2008 South Ossetia war is complicated. After years of escalating tension, Georgia's assault on Tskhinvali catalysed full scale fighting with the northern bear. Russia's response pushed far beyond South Ossetia into the rest of Georgia. Russian forces remain in South Ossetia and Abkhazia today - an occupation of a previous part of Georgia.

As we climb into dark rain clouds as we head towards the Russian border, we arrive to this massive stone semi-circle - the Russia–Georgia Friendship Monument. There is a bleak little joke in arriving there on a bus full of Russian visitors in a divided country.

To me, that war looks like a warning of what was to come in Ukraine. Not a neat rehearsal, but an early demonstration of how little protection a small country's borders offer when a much larger neighbour decides its interests matter more. Russia annexed Crimea in 2014, then launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022. Georgia had already lived with the consequences of that logic for years.

And still, here was my bus. Russian tourists could buy a seat to the mountains. Yet Georgia recorded nearly 1.6 million visitor trips from Russia in 2025. The scale is there. A Russian holidaymaker is not the Russian government. A shop taking their money is not voting for Moscow. People can resent the power to the north and still need to deal with the people coming from it. The contradiction is only simple when you're far away.

At Gergeti, the church sits above Stepantsminda with Mkinvartsveri, Mount Kazbegi, rising behind it. This is the photograph I came north for. The mountain's northern slope is in Russia.

But the photograph cannot tell you everything about the road that brought us here. It cannot tell you what the Georgians who sold me a ticket thought about the Russian tourists sitting beside me. I don't know, and I won't pretend to.

I took the cheaper seat because it got me here. I came for the mountain and the church. The bus and the road turned out to be part of the picture too.
